Dear Yasmine Omri,

I read Agent Memory: Characterization and System Implications of Stateful
Long-Horizon Workloads in full. I run a memory system in continuous production
and instrumented it along phases close to yours. I am writing to offer the data,
not to ask for anything.

The overlap is partial, and the difference is why I am writing. We share
construction and retrieval. We do not measure generation — our telemetry only
sees calls the memory system itself makes, not the agents' own LLM turns. In
exchange we attribute a phase your harness does not: maintenance, the stage your
Section 2.1 defines and then notes is weak or absent in many current systems.

The system serves six agents on a single VPS. Over the 32 complete days from
7 August, every one of which has data, the shared store logged 3,544 provider
calls costing USD 0.1352 in total: construction is 3.16% of operations and 55.7%
of cost, query 94.6% and 30.5%, maintenance 2.3% and 13.8%.

The reason behind that split is more useful than the split, and it is a caution
about phase-level cost attribution generally. Per thousand input tokens the
three phases are indistinguishable — 0.000122, 0.000150 and 0.000157 USD. The
entire difference is input size: 5,500 tokens on an average construction call
against 82 on a query. And the aggregate rests on very few events. Three digest
calls, of 142k, 125k and 125k input tokens, are 0.08% of operations and 29.7% of
all cost; dropping just those three moves construction from 55.7% to 37.0%. Half
the total spend sits in 31 calls, 0.87% of the series.

I do not think your harness could have surfaced that, and the reason is in your
protocol rather than your instrumentation: histories are streamed in fixed
4096-token chunks to match MAB, so the per-call input size distribution is
constant by construction and cannot exhibit a tail. The Section 4.7 sweep does
vary input, but along total history size for one user, which is a different axis
— it moves how much is built, not how unevenly each call is sized. In production
the two come apart, and phase-level cost tracks the size distribution rather
than the phase.

Your Recommendation 6 conditions amortization on high-volume query workloads
against stable histories. Neither condition holds here, so I cannot test it: a
hundred queries a day is not high volume, and the history is not stable —
construction only runs when documents change, by design, which lands on 19 of
the 32 days, maintenance on 11.

Your Recommendation 9 asks operators to add independent pruning or forgetting
policies, since none of the evaluated systems prune or forget by default. This
system has one — retention windows typed per content class, salience decay,
epoch and graph pruning — and, more usefully, the record of it failing: a
compounding error in the decay drained the knowledge graph from about 21,500
nodes to 554 before it was diagnosed and recovered.

That policy also puts pressure on one axis of your taxonomy. In Table 1
mutability travels with agent control: the three systems marked mutate are all
Paradigm IV. Here the store mutates, deletes, decays and prunes with no LLM in
the loop — maintenance is deterministic cron. If that is a real position rather
than a mislabel on my part, mutability and control may be separable axes rather
than one.

I can share the telemetry as a dataset with its schema. It holds no query text,
only hashes, so there is nothing to scrub. The forgetting-policy post-mortem
also exists, if the operational side of Recommendation 9 is of interest. None of
these numbers appear in the technical report on the system, which predates the
instrumentation; it is at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22649269 if the
architecture is worth a look.
